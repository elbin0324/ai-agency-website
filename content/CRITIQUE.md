# Buyer critique of the copy

Three skeptical readers reviewed content/COPY.md (12-person dental practice owner, COO of a 180-person distributor, marketing lead at a 40-person e-commerce brand). Page builders: apply the edits and missing content that touch your sections.

## Reader reactions

(a) Owner of a 12-person dental practice (non-technical, skeptical of AI). WINS: The voice suits her. It is calm with no hype, and candid lines such as "If AI isn't the right fix, we'll tell you on the first call" and "We'll never ask for passwords or system access on the call" lower her guard. The dental card in Industries ("fill cancellations from the waitlist, so the front desk can focus on the people in the room") describes her week, and the AI Agents H1 lands on "front desk". Three things ease her two big fears, patients feeling tricked and staff feeling threatened: the Customer Agent demo's safety check and clean handoff, "Introduces itself as a virtual assistant", and the answer to "Will AI replace my employees?". A free call with a written plan either way is the right offer. LOSES HER: (1) HIPAA is never mentioned. There is no business associate agreement, and data rules are agreed "with your compliance lead", which a 12-person practice doesn't have. For her this alone ends the visit. (2) "AI agents" is never explained on the home page. The only definition is in FAQ 2, which sits in section 12 of 13. The text she skims is IT and marketing shorthand: least-privilege access, audit logs, "tools with an API", "a hypothesis and a stopping rule", "capacity freed", FTE, "approvals switched on". (3) Nothing is her size. Every dental example is a four-office group of 50 to 60 people: the Willowmere scenario, and the Stillbrook demo with $24,000 of ads every four weeks. The Customer Agent demo is an HVAC company, and the one dental demo is labeled "Marketing Director", so she'd never open it. (4) "Can it work with our practice software? Often, yes... a shared calendar your team confirms" sounds like "maybe, and more work for my front desk", and no practice-management system is named. (5) No prices plus mid-size language (Partner plans, executive reporting, leadership planning sessions) makes her assume it's priced for bigger firms. The pilot ("people approve its output until the quality is consistently where you want it") sounds like extra work for stretched staff. (6) She wants a face and a phone number and gets a 13-field form and an email address. Half the offer (sales outreach, ad testing, P&L consulting) is irrelevant to her and makes the firm feel like a generalist. The 16-name integrations strip and the comparison against "generic automation agencies" she has never considered don't earn their place for her. BEFORE BOOKING SHE NEEDS: whether they'll sign a BAA, whether it works with her practice software, how pricing works for a practice her size, who exactly she'd deal with, and how much staff time the pilot takes.

(b) COO of a 180-person distribution company (ROI- and risk-focused). WINS: The methodology is the strongest thing on the site, and it is how he'd build the case himself: loaded costs from BLS data, 20% set aside for human review, only half of negotiated savings counted, gross profit instead of revenue, "Hours aren't dollars until they are", and "Before our fees... your proposal shows net savings and payback". The pilot design (baseline and success measure in writing, then go, adjust or stop), the four autonomy levels, audit logs, read-only books and the per-phase outputs in Approach 10.3 read like a vendor that has run projects. Three pieces are directly relevant: the Analyst Agent demo (a distributor's margin slipping on freight), the Ironvale scenario (quote turnaround, margin floors, lapsed accounts) and the consulting deliverables (an opportunity register and a model he keeps). "We say what isn't worth automating" earns trust. LOSES HIM: (1) There's no pricing structure, so he can't put a budget line in front of his CEO or estimate net ROI. He can't tell whether there's a setup fee, whether it's monthly or per project, whether AI usage is included or passed through, or how long the contract runs. "It depends on scope" plus "before our fees" leaves the bottom line blank. (2) Vendor risk goes unanswered. This is a self-described "young firm" with no named people, team size, location, insurance or SOC 2 statement. "We describe our practices plainly instead of pointing to badges" reads as a dodge to his IT lead, and nothing answers "what if you go away?". (3) "Accountable for results" turns out to mean monthly reporting. There's no support window, no incident notification time (Approach 10.5 says "tell you promptly"), and nothing on who pays for a wrong quote to a key account. (4) The integrations strip is small-business software (QuickBooks, Xero, Stripe) with no ERP, EDI or warehouse system, so he concludes it's built for 15-person shops. The Partner model is one short card. (5) He will catch the inconsistencies. The calculator copy describes four inputs with 4.33-week and 173-hour constants, but SCENARIOS.md has five inputs, 4 weeks and 160 hours. The copy promises "four worked examples" against six examples plus six scenarios. Demo businesses are named differently in COPY.md and DEMOS.md. A consulting demo tile says "2 days vs about 4 to 6 weeks" next to "typically 3 to 5 weeks". One mismatch discounts every number. (6) "The depth of a consulting engagement, at a fraction of the cost" is asserted twice and never explained, and "a person checks every finding" never says who that person is or what qualifies them. Overclaims he'll mark: "does all the Excel", "The pipeline fills without another hire", and "Together, they share what they learn", which also raises a data question. BEFORE BOOKING HE NEEDS: the pricing model, a security packet (providers used, data location, SOC 2 status, insurance), ERP compatibility, named people and support terms, and a sample pilot report.

(c) Marketing lead at a 40-person e-commerce brand. WINS: The Marketing page shows rigor she respects: "Tracking you can't trust", "Calls winners on evidence", one change at a time with a hypothesis and a stopping rule, a change log with rollback, caps set inside the ad platforms, approval for new messaging and a list of approved claims. The Stillbrook Dental demo is the most persuasive thing on the site for her: win probabilities, a ±20% weekly budget guardrail, and "No patient data is sent to ad platforms". The Sales page's comparison of a generic email with a researched one proves the writing quality. "Spend less to win each customer." is a strong H1, and the minimum-spend FAQ is candid. LOSES HER: (1) The product is called "AI Marketing Director", it "can run your accounts end to end", and the answer to "Does this replace my agency or marketer?" is "It doesn't have to". She is usually the one evaluating it, and the copy hands her a reason to argue against it. (2) The page is written for local lead generation: cost per lead, booked jobs, "spend outside your area or hours", search terms, LinkedIn. It says nothing about return on ad spend judged against contribution margin, new-customer acquisition cost, Shopping or Performance Max, Meta Advantage+, TikTok, product feeds, or Google and Meta both claiming the same order. (3) Email and SMS lifecycle marketing (Klaviyo flows, abandoned cart, post-purchase) drives a large share of her revenue, and it isn't offered or mentioned anywhere. (4) "Drafts new creative" covers headlines and copy. She can't tell who produces the images and video that actually move Meta results. (5) The Customer Agent is described in terms of appointments, quotes and technicians. Order status, returns and exchanges in Shopify, where her support load lives, appear only in the Fernway scenario on the Results page. (6) She can't tell whether customer lists get uploaded to ad platforms, because the dental demo's guardrail never reaches the copy. The Sales Agent and Consulting pages read as B2B and local services, not her world. BEFORE BOOKING SHE NEEDS: an e-commerce example on the Marketing page, a clear statement of her role (she decides; it executes and reports), which platforms are supported, a sample weekly report, and the customer-data guardrails.

Across all three: the honesty system (labels, visible math, "what isn't counted", no invented proof) is a real differentiator, so keep it. The questions readers need answered before booking are covered unevenly. What they must provide is well covered (Approach 10.7, Home FAQ 3). Time to go live is clear, but it doesn't say when they'll feel a difference. Pricing structure is missing. "What if it's wrong" lists safeguards but not the response, the remedy or who is liable. Data safety lists practices but not who at Halyard sees the data, which providers get it, SOC 2 status or a BAA. Accountability points to a "named lead" who is never named. The site also buries its best material: 13 home sections with about 45 cards, 10 FAQs and a table, "Book a free AI assessment" 27 times, and the "no case studies" disclaimer repeated in Home FAQ 9 and Results 9.5. Sections that don't earn their place: Services 3.4 "Better together" (vague cross-sell), Approach 10.2 "Principles" (restates the pillars) and Home outcomes group 2 (repeats the services). Weak headlines: "Made for local businesses and growing mid-size companies.", "Clear deliverables you can act on.", "Five rules we work by.", "No invented proof." and "What's included.". On the consulting idea: comparing with traditional consulting is the right instinct, and the COO would respond to it. Today, though, "a fraction of the cost" is a bare claim, the demo's own timing contradicts it, and there's no fixed price to point to. It needs three things: the reason it's cheaper (AI does the analyst hours a firm would bill for), a side-by-side comparison, and timing that agrees across the page (edits 5, 7 and 11). Naming firms such as McKinsey would be a straw man for this buyer and would need sourced fees, so compare against the kinds of firms they actually hire.

## Edits

1. **§2.12 Home FAQ, Q6 "How much does it cost?" (reuse the same answer on /approach/#faq)** (content/COPY.md). All three readers want to know the shape of the bill before they give up 30 minutes, and the answer offers only "it depends on scope." The dental owner can't tell a few hundred dollars a month from a five-figure project and assumes it's priced for bigger firms. The COO can't put a budget line in front of his CEO without knowing whether there's a setup fee or a monthly fee, whether usage is charged, and how long the contract runs. The brief bans prices, not pricing structure. SCENARIOS.md already defines a price-free yardstick (the break-even budget) that COPY.md never uses. Founder decision: confirm each structural term (fixed-price pilot, flat monthly fee including AI usage, pass-through costs) and add it to Appendix A.

   Replacement: **How much does it cost?**
We don't publish prices because scope drives them, but here's how pricing works. A pilot is a fixed price for a fixed scope, agreed in writing before any work starts. If you continue, managed agents are a flat monthly fee that covers monitoring, upkeep, changes within scope and AI usage at the volumes we agreed. Third-party costs, like phone minutes or a software seat, are listed in the proposal at cost. There's no long-term contract to start, and every proposal puts our fee next to the modeled savings, so you see the payback before you sign. Want a yardstick now? The savings calculator shows your break-even budget: the most an AI program could cost each month and still pay for itself in time saved.

   Replacement: **What if an agent gets something wrong?**

Agents answer only from information you approve, anything unclear goes to a person, and early on your team approves the work before it goes out, which is where most mistakes get caught. If one slips through, we tell you the same business day, correct it with the customer or in the record, pause that kind of task if needed and fix the cause. You'll see what happened and what changed in the next report. Your agreement spells out who is responsible for what, and we carry professional liability insurance. Every action is logged, so there's never a question about what the agent did or why.

Approach §10.5 card 7: **Incident response.** If something goes wrong, we pause the affected agent, tell you the same business day, fix the cause and give you a written account of what happened and what changed.
